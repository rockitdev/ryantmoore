> An agent that runs your work is not free labour. It is a worker you rent by the minute, and the meter never stops. That changes which jobs are worth handing over.

There is a story going around that a company crossed ten million dollars in yearly revenue with nobody on the payroll. One founder, a stack of AI agents, and a nightly routine where the software picks the next thing to do. It is a real business, and it is a real number.

Here is the part that does not make the headline. The bill for all that thinking ran to seven figures in a single month. Tasks the founder priced at a dollar sometimes cost thirty or fifty in fees behind the scenes. The work got done while he slept. It also cost him real money every time it woke up.

## The meter is always running

When you hire a person, you pay them whether it is a busy day or a slow one. An agent is the opposite. It costs you nothing when it sits still, and it costs you every single time it runs. Every check, every draft, every "let me look at that again" is another few cents, and the cents add up fast when the thing runs all day.

That is not a reason to avoid it. It is a reason to be picky. The math only works when the job you hand over is worth more than the meter it runs on.

## Where the math works

The jobs worth automating share a shape. They repeat, they follow a pattern, and skipping one costs you something real.

- The follow-up that should go out every time a lead comes in. Miss it and you lose the job.
- The nightly summary that tells you what happened with your customers while you were off the clock.
- The invoice that should land in the books without anyone retyping it.

Each of those has a clear price on getting it wrong. A missed lead is a lost job. A late invoice is money that sits uncollected. When the cost of the mistake is bigger than the cost of the run, the agent earns its keep.

## Where it does not

The jobs that burn money quietly are the ones that run constantly and do not change much for you either way. An agent that re-checks the same thing every five minutes, or writes ten versions of an email you were only going to skim, is a worker you are paying to look busy. It feels productive. It is just expensive.

The trap is that agents are eager. Left alone, they will happily do more than the job needs, and you do not see the bill until the end of the month.

## How to think about it

Before you hand a job to something that runs on its own, ask two plain questions. What does it cost me when this does not happen? And how often does it really need to run to cover that?

If the answer is "a lot" and "not that often," you have found a good job for an agent. If it is "not much" and "constantly," keep it on a person, or on yourself.

The company with no employees and the seven-figure bill is not a cautionary tale. It is a pricing lesson. The agents are worth it right up until they are not, and the whole skill is knowing where that line sits for your business.
