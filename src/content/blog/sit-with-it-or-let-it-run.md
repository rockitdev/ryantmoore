> Most AI mistakes I see in small businesses aren't about the tool being weak. They're about using the sit-with-it kind for a job that needed the runs-on-its-own kind, or the other way around.

There are really only two kinds of AI helper worth caring about in a business. Once you can tell them apart, most of the confusing choices get simple.

## The one you sit with

The first kind is an assistant you steer. You open it, you tell it what you want, you watch it work, and you correct it as it goes. Think of the AI you already type into. It's fast and it's smart, but it only does anything while you're sitting there with it. Close the laptop and it stops.

This kind is great for **thinking work**. Drafting a proposal. Digging through a messy spreadsheet. Figuring out a plan. Work that's a little different every time and where you want a human hand on the wheel.

It's the wrong tool the moment you catch yourself wishing it would "just keep doing that." It can't. It waits for you.

## The one that runs on its own

The second kind is an agent that works unattended. You set it up once, and then it wakes up on a schedule or when something happens, does the job, and puts the result where it needs to go. No one has to be watching. It runs while you're asleep, on a job site, or on vacation.

This kind is for **the repeating stuff**. The follow-up that should go out every time a lead comes in. The nightly summary of what happened with your customers. The invoice that should land in the books without anyone retyping it. Work that's the same shape over and over, and that quietly costs you when a busy human forgets.

## How to tell which one a job needs

You don't need to know any of the technology to make the call. Ask a few plain questions about the task:

- **Does it happen on a schedule, or only when someone decides to do it?** On a schedule points to the runs-on-its-own kind.
- **Does it need to happen when nobody's at a computer?** If yes, it has to run on its own.
- **Does a person need to check or approve it every single time?** If yes, keep a human in the loop.
- **Is the job still changing, or is it settled?** Still figuring it out means sit with it. Settled and repeating means let it run.
- **Does the result need to land in your other tools automatically?** If yes, that's a job for the agent that runs on its own.

Most tasks answer these quickly, and the right choice falls out.

## The part people get wrong

Two mistakes cost the most.

The first is treating the sit-with-it assistant like it's automation. "I use AI for that" often means a person is still opening it, prompting it, and copying the answer somewhere by hand every time. That's not automation. That's a faster version of doing it yourself, and it stops the day that person gets busy.

The second is letting an unattended agent loose on important work with no guardrails. Something that runs on its own, touching your money or your customers, needs a check step and a clear line it isn't allowed to cross. The freedom that makes it useful is the same freedom that makes it dangerous without rails.

## The move that actually works

The pattern I keep coming back to: **prove it while you're sitting with it, then set it loose.**

Start a new task with the assistant you steer. Get it right while you can watch and correct. Once it's boring and repeatable, that's your signal to hand it to the kind that runs on its own, with a guardrail or two so it stays safe. You keep the judgment. It takes the repetition.

Truth is, most businesses don't need more AI. They need to quit using the sit-with-it kind for work that should just run itself, and quit trusting the runs-on-its-own kind with the calls that need a human. That's the whole fix.
